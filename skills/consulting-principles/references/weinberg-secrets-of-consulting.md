## Chapter 2

If logic always worked, nobody would need consultants, so consultants always confront contradictions. "Don't be rational, be reasonable"

Optimitis and the tradeoff treatment:

“Optimitis can be found in anyone who is asked to produce solutions to problems. It is an inﬂammation of the optimization nerve, that part of the nervous system which responds to such requests as
"Give us the minimum cost solution."
"Get it done in the shortest possible time."
"We must do it in the best possible way."
In a healthy individual, the optimization nerve receives such requests and sends an impulse to the mouth to respond,"

“What are you willing to sacriﬁce?"

In the diseased individual, however, this neural pathway is interrupted, and the mouth utters some distorted phrases like,
"Yes, boss. Right away, boss.”

      The answer to optimitis is to identify the tradeoff -- "You don't get nothin' for nothin'."

Fisher's Fundamental Theorem -- "The better adapted you are, the less adaptable you tend to be."

Risk vs Certainty

“Consultants are less adapted to the present situation, and therefore are potentially more adaptable. Their perception of now/then tradeoffs is different from those close to the problem, which makes them a valuable source of ideas, as well as people not to be trusted.”

“By working with a client for an extended period of time, it's possible to establish trust by recommending only low-risk alternatives. This strategy is another now/then tradeoff: small results now for the possibility of bigger results later. But later, the consultant will be better adapted to the situation, and thus less likely to provide a truly big idea.
These consulting tradeoffs may explain something I've observed in myself and other consultants:
Consultants tend to be most effective on the third problem you give them.”

The orange juice test -- “We can do it—and this is how much it will cost.” -- answering either "yes" or "no" to a request for work isn't enough information.

## Chapter 3 -- Being effective when you don't know what you're doing

Three times out of four, consultants are asked to work on problems outside their specialty. They need a back of generic problem solving tricks to work on these.

Marvin's secrets of medicine:
  The first great secret: "Ninety percent of all illness cures itself -- with absolutely no intervention from the doctor"
      Adapted for consulting: "Deal gently with systems that should be able to cure themselves"
      The first law of engineering: "If it ain't broke don't fix it"

  Marvin's second secret: "Repeatedly curing a system that can cure itself will create a system that can't"

“This secret should be branded on the bottom of every parent who ever wiped the nose of a child over the age of four, and on the forehead of any consultant who makes a living solving the same problem for the same client, over and over.”

  Marvin's third secret: "Every prescription has two parts: the medicine and the method of ensuring correct use"

  Marvin's fourth secret: "If what they've been doing hasn't solved the problem, tell them to do something else"

  The fifth great secret: "Make sure they're paying you well enough so they'll do what you say."
      Said another way: "The most important act in consulting is charging the right fee"

  The sixth secret: "Know-how pays much less than know-when"

The Bolden rule: "If you can't fix it, feature it"
    Ex: If you've got a long turnaround time, call it "think time"

The gilded rule: "If you can't feature it, fake it" -- Ex: a neighbourhood named Oakmont Cascade will likely not contain either oak, mountain or cascade.

The inverse gilded rule: "If something's faked, it must need fixing"

Bugs are just mistakes. Someone applied the gilded rule. Clients who use euphemisms are hiding something, usually from themselves

## Chapter 4 -- Seeing what's there

The law of the hammer --  “The child who receives a hammer for Christmas will discover
that everything needs pounding.”

You're in danger if your repertoire is narrow

The white bread warning -- "If you use the same recipe, you get the same bread" -- try to learn how things got the way they are so you don't go backwards

Boulding's backward Basis -- "Things are the way they are because they got that way"
   History is important, but can be boring and irrelevant. Learn it anyway.
   Things are the way they are because of a system.

Sparks' law of problem Solution -- The chances of solving the problem decline the closer you get to finding out to finding out who was the cause of the problem.

      * my paraphrase: Blame doesn't help

Keep history simple and not too detailed.
Study for understanding, not for criticism.
Look for what you like in the current situation, and comment on it. (Keep people talking)

The Why Whammy -- We may run out of energy, or air, or water, or food, but we'll never run out of reasons
         -- Review comment: You can pummel people with why questions, but it's usefulness is limited. People get defensive when they're pummelled

The Label Law -- Most of us buy the label, not the merchandise.
   The name of the thing is not the thing.

The Three Finger Rule -- When you point a finger at someone, notice where the other three fingers are pointing.

"Overrun" and "underbudgeted" are two terms that apply to the same situation. It's just blame. Labeling it as overrun assumes the budget was correct, labelling it as underbudgeted assumes the work was done as efficiently as possible. Managers tend to speak of overruns because it minimizes their contribution to the problem, workers speak of underfunding for the same reason.

“Attaching an emotionally charged label to direct attention away from one aspect of a situation is called The Misdirection Method”

The five minute rule -- “Clients always know how to solve their problems, and always tell the solution in the ﬁrst ﬁve minutes.”

## Chapter 5 -- Seeing what's not there

The level law -- Effective problem solvers may have many problems, but rarely have a single, dominant problem.

If you as a consultant ﬁnd a relatively even distribution of problems, you may hypothesize that your clients are not seeing one major problem, but it is more likely that they have been keeping up with their problems without letting any one problem get out of control.

*** Just read this chapter. It isn't as super-duper-quotable as the others, but is still good, especially for short engagements where finding problems quickly is helpful ***

The rule of three -- If you can't think of three things that might go wrong with your plans, then there's something wrong with your thinking

    If you can't think of anything, use brainstorming.
    Try analogies, move to extremes, look outside boundaries, look for alabis vs explanations (ready written standards to look for documented guidelines as band-aids over existing problems)

The incongruence insight -- “When words and music don't go together, they point to a missing element." (watch for body language that does not match words)

Brown's brilliant bequest -- Words are often useful, but it always pays to listen to the music (especially your own internal music).

## Chapter 10 -- Resistance

If you don't encounter any resistance, our ides don't get tested. Nobody's perfect. Appreciate resistance.

Learn to appreciate your own behavioural cues and reactions to resistance. You may notice these before your conscious mind catches on.

Name the resistance in a neutral way, locate the nature of the resistance

## Chapter 11 -- Marketing your services

    Laws of marketing
    1. A consultant can exist in one of two states: State I (Idle), or State B(Busy)  (There is no such thing as "just the right amount of business")
    2. The best way to get clients is to have clients
    3. Spend at least one day a week getting exposure (since the best time to get clients is when you have clients, you have to devote some time for marketing when you're busy)
    4. Clients are more important to you than you can ever be for them
        1. $10k in expense seems like a lot to you, but it's nothing to a $10B/year client
        2. No budget cut is easier than cutting a consultant
        3. In other words, pay attention to law #3
    5. Never let a single client have more than 1/4 of your business
        1. Most people can continue indefinitely on 3/4 of their present income
    6. The best marketing tool is a satisfied client
        1. They're the best prospective future clients
        2. Having references triples your chances of new contracts
    7. Give away your best ideas
    8. The Duncan Hines Difference: it tastes better when you add your own egg
        1. As long as consumers contribute something themselves, the "egg" can be almost anything
        2. Having the answers to everything drives clients away
    9. Spend at least 1/4 of your time doing nothing
        1. If you also follow law #5, your billable time is only half of your actual available time
    10. Market for quality, not quantity (Charlotte's Law)
        1. Marketing questions for successful marketers go from "How do I get more business" to "Do I really want to do this?"

    Lynne's law of life
        To be able to say yes to yourself as a consultant, be able to say no to any of your clients

    Consulting is a high risk business

    Be in the idea business, not the lawsuit business. Keep generating new ideas, dont' worry about the stolen ones


## Chapter 12 -- Putting a price on your head

    Pricing has many functions, only one of which is the exchange of money

    The more they pay you, the more they love you

    The less they pay you, the less they respect you

        (Does this apply to our consultancy? We're often one degree removed from pricing)

    Clients that offer you more money tend to give you more leeway to do whatever you think they need. Clients that offer you less money tend to tie your hands

    The money is usually the smallest part of the price
        The total cost of your services includes the emotional cost of admitting there is a problem, the labor to get approval for your visit, the difficulty of changing schedules, etc. If you don't acknowledge this, you may end up raising the price to more than they can pay.

    Pricing is not a zero sum game
        Clients can show you around the city, or let you use their facilities etc. They have things they can provide you at near zero cost that have value to you

    If you need the money, don't take the job
        You might charge too much since you need it, or too low to get the job. Both of these occurrences destroy the usefulness of pricing as a consulting tool

    If they don't like your work, don't take their money

    Price is more than money, but it's also true that money is more than price
        You can use money to create the conditions you need for success
        Use an advance payment to encourage clients to do prep work
        Use a deposit if the client needs to secure a specific date and might change their mind
        etc.

    Pricing is not a thing, it's a negotiated relationship

    Set price so you won't regret it either way
        if they accept, you're getting paid. If they reject it, you didn't really want the job anyway, so you set a price that made it worthwhile

    All prices are ultimately based on feelings, both yours and theirs

## Chapter 13 -- How to be trusted

    The first law of trust: Nobody but you cares about the reason you let another person down
        Whether its' bad luck or that you lied just does not matter.

    The second law of trust: Trust takes years to win, moments to lose

    The third law of trust: People don't tell you when they stop trusting you
        This makes it very difficult to correct your own behaviour that the client sees as untrustworthy

    The fourth law of trust: The trick of earning trust is to avoid all tricks

    The fifth law of trust: People are never liars -- in their own eyes
    The sixth law of trust: Always trust your client -- and cut the cards
        Trust them, but verify what they tell you. Maybe you misunderstood, or that someone misunderstood what was asked.

    The seventh law of trust: Never be dishonest, even if the client requests it.
    The eighth law of trust: Never promise anything
    The ninth law of trust: Always keep your promise
    The tenth law of trust: Get it in writing, but depend on trust

    The final law of trust is the golden rule. (Just like the 11th commandment)
